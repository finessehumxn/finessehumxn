01

Landing

Sunday, August 30 · California to Atlanta to Lima · the day you lose to travel, and the hotel mistake

Every trip has a day that is not really a day. This was ours. It is on here anyway, because the two things that went wrong on it are the two things I would tell anyone flying into Lima.
All day

Delta One, ATL to LIMThe flight

California to Atlanta to Jorge Chávez International

Two legs. West coast to Atlanta, then Atlanta straight down to Lima. Delta One is the lie flat cabin, which matters more than usual on this route because the Lima leg lands you into a full day you are expected to function in.

Fun fact

Lima runs on UTC-5 year round with no daylight saving, so from the west coast it is only a two hour shift. There is no real jet lag on this trip. What wrecks you is the flight length, not the clock.

Must know

Jorge Chávez opened an entirely new terminal in June 2025 and it is still shaking out the kinks. Build a real buffer on connections, and check your flight status the night before and the morning of.

Must know

Do not book the airport hotel unless your layover is genuinely a few hours. A late arrival is not a reason. Eat the ride, get to Miraflores or Barranco the same night, and wake up somewhere you actually want to be. We lost most of a day undoing this.

Fun fact

The airport sits in Callao, which is its own port city, not Lima proper. Nothing around it is where you want to spend a night. The good districts are all 45 minutes south.

Dinner

KFC, and the delivery app problemThe lesson

Ordered in

First night in one of the great food cities on earth and we ate KFC in a hotel room. That is what a travel day does to you, and there is no shame in it. What there is, is a logistics lesson nobody warns you about.

Must know

UberEats does not operate in Peru. Uber for rides works perfectly, which is exactly why this catches people out. For food delivery you need Rappi or PedidosYa. Download both at the airport while you still have wifi, before you are hungry and stuck.

Do this

Rappi is the one that covers the most restaurants in Lima and it also delivers groceries and pharmacy items. Set it up first. PedidosYa is the backup when a place is not on Rappi.

Fun fact

It is built from millions of hand made bricks stood on end like books on a shelf, not stacked flat. That bookshelf pattern is why it has survived centuries of earthquakes. The gaps let it shake without collapsing.

La PicanteríaThe big lunch

Splurge

Francisco Moreno 388, Surquillo

Héctor Solís brought northern Peruvian cooking to Lima and built it around one idea. You walk in, you look at the whole fish on ice, you pick one, and the kitchen turns that single fish into three or four different dishes for the table. Communal tables, tile floors, zero pretension, serious food.

Pick one whole fish for the table and let them split it. Ceviche first, then sudado or a fried preparation. Add the tortilla de raya, a stingray omelette that sounds alarming and tastes like the best crab cake of your life. Chicha de jora to drink.

Fun fact

A picantería is a northern Peruvian institution, historically a house where a woman cooked and sold food and chicha out of her own kitchen. Solís built a famous restaurant by refusing to make it fancier than that.

Portions are enormous and priced by fish weight, so ask the price of the fish before you nod at it. The upstairs balcony is up a staircase, so ask for a ground floor table.

What to actually order in Peru

Fifteen words of a
Peruvian menu.

Heat is marked, because ají is used constantly here and some of it lands harder than it looks.

CevicheHas heat
Raw fish cured in lime with red onion, chili and salt, served with sweet potato and giant corn. The lime liquid left in the bowl is leche de tigre and you drink it. Ask for it sin ají if anyone wants it mild.
TiraditoMilder
Ceviche's calmer cousin. Fish sliced thin like sashimi, sauced rather than marinated, no onion. This is the one to order for a parent who is unsure about raw fish.
CausaMild
Chilled layers of yellow potato whipped with lime and ají amarillo, packed around chicken, tuna or crab. Looks like a small cake. Tastes nothing like one.
Lomo saltadoMild
Beef stir fried with onion, tomato and soy in a screaming hot wok, served over fries and rice. Chinese technique, Peruvian ingredients. The safest bet on any menu.
Ají de gallinaGentle
Shredded chicken in a creamy yellow chili and walnut sauce over rice. Comfort food, warm rather than hot.
AnticuchosHas heat
Beef heart, marinated and grilled on skewers over coals. It does not taste like organ meat, it tastes like the best steak tip you have had. The street food of Lima.
Rocoto rellenoGenuinely spicy
A rocoto pepper stuffed with spiced beef and cheese and baked. The pepper is the whole dish and it is hot. Order one for the table to try, not one each.
Papa a la huancaínaMild
Boiled potato under a cold, creamy cheese and yellow chili sauce. Almost every meal starts with this somewhere.
Arroz chaufaMild
Peruvian Chinese fried rice, born in Lima's Barrio Chino. Order it at a chifa and understand half of Lima instantly.
Pan con chicharrónMild
Fried pork, sweet potato and salsa criolla in a roll. The national hangover breakfast, sold before noon and rarely after.
PicaronesSweet
Squash and sweet potato doughnut rings, fried and drenched in chancaca syrup. Street dessert. Eat them standing up.
Suspiro a la limeñaSweet
Caramel manjar blanco under a port spiked meringue. The name means the sigh of a woman from Lima, which tells you exactly how sweet it is.
Pisco sourStrong
Pisco, lime, syrup, egg white, bitters. Peru's national drink and a genuine argument with Chile over who invented it. Stronger than it tastes.
ChilcanoEasy
Pisco, ginger ale, lime, ice. Lighter than a pisco sour and much easier to drink with food. This is what locals order on a weeknight.
Chicha moradaNo alcohol
Purple corn boiled with pineapple, clove and cinnamon, then chilled. Sweet, spiced, non alcoholic, and the correct drink with ceviche.
Inca KolaNo alcohol
Bright yellow, tastes like bubblegum, and outsells Coca-Cola in Peru so thoroughly that Coke eventually gave up and bought a stake in it. Try it once for the story.
The market list

Ranked by whether it is
worth your time.

Five markets came up. They are not equivalent, and two of them are not a good use of three remaining days.

Inka Market, Miraflores

Go. Av. Petit Thouars, blocks 5200 to 5400. Artisan goods, alpaca, silver, ceramics, all in a safe walkable strip. Already built into Day 3 because it shares a street with ChocoMuseo.

Bargain to about 70 percent of the opening price. Bundle purchases at one stall.

1.2 MI · 9 MIN

Barrio Chino

Go if you want lunch, not shopping. Calle Capón in the historic center. Lima's Chinatown is where chifa was invented, which is arguably the most influential thing that has ever happened to Peruvian food.

Daytime only, and go for a chifa meal with arroz chaufa rather than for the shops. Watch your bag on Capón.

~5 MI · 25 MIN

Polvos Azules

Only if you love a chaotic bazaar. An enormous informal market in the center selling electronics, clothing and a great deal of counterfeit everything. It is an experience, not a shopping trip.

Cash only in practice, crowded, and pickpocketing is the main risk. Nothing you buy here has a warranty.

~5 MI · 25 MIN

Gamarra, La Victoria

Skip on this trip. South America's largest garment district, tens of thousands of textile businesses in a few dozen blocks. Fascinating if you are sourcing clothing. Overwhelming if you are not.

La Victoria requires real street awareness, and it will eat an entire day you do not have.

~4 MI · 30 MIN

Plaza Norte

Done. You already made the nine mile, near hour long drive to Independencia. That was the mall trip. There is no reason to make that drive twice.

9 MI · ~60 MIN
Cut from the list, and why

Being honest about
what does not fit.

That is the difference between an itinerary and a wish list.

Laguna Esmeralda, Huacho

This one hurts, because the photos are unreal. But it is at kilometer 137 of the Panamericana Norte, roughly three hours each way from Lima. That is six hours in a car out of a three day trip. Worse, it is not a public natural site. Access runs through a private condominium development, departures are limited and decided by them, and multiple visitors report that the tour includes a pitch to sell you a plot of land. With parents and three days left, this is a no.

If the sandboarding is the part you want, the Chilca dunes are about an hour south of Miraflores, tours run at 8 AM and 2 PM daily, and operators will dial the dune buggy down to a gentle version on request. That is a half day instead of a full one.

Helarte

Calle Bolívar 205, open daily 8 AM to 10 PM, and you already went. Worth knowing it is 0.4 miles from the lobby and open until 10 PM, which makes it the default late dessert on any night that ends early. Lúcuma is the flavor to get if you did not.

Money

About S/3.37 to the dollar. Cards work at every restaurant on this list. Carry small soles for taxis, markets, the fountains and the tiny places.

Tipping is about 10 percent, and check the bill for servicio first, because if it is there you do not add more.

Water

Do not drink the tap water, including in the hotel. Sealed bottles only. Ice at real restaurants is fine. At street stalls and juice carts, ask for it without ice.

Getting around

Uber and Cabify both work well here and are cheap. Book through the app rather than hailing on the street, especially at night and especially outside a busy attraction.

Every drive on this itinerary is under 35 minutes except the one to Plaza Norte, which is why it is not on it twice.

Food delivery, the trap

UberEats does not operate in Peru. Uber for rides works perfectly, which is exactly what makes people assume the food app does too.

Download Rappi first and PedidosYa as the backup. Do it at the airport on wifi, not at 10 PM when you are hungry.

Where to stay

Miraflores. Not the airport. The airport hotel is attached to the terminal and that is its only argument.

We booked it for the arrival night and left immediately. Eat the 45 minute ride and wake up somewhere worth waking up in.

Staying smart

Miraflores, Barranco and San Isidro are the comfortable districts and this itinerary lives almost entirely inside them.

Phone away on quiet side streets, bag in front on crowded ones, and take a car back from the center rather than walking after dark.

Weather

Around 60 to 68 degrees, grey, and damp on the coast. September is the tail of the garúa season. Bring one layer everywhere. It will not rain in any meaningful way.

Two useful phrases

La cuenta, por favor to get the bill, which never comes until you ask. Sin ají if someone wants it without the chili.

Take the whole thing. Change the city.

Distance from where you are sleeping, what it costs, what to order, and the one thing nobody tells you. It works for Lima. It works for everywhere else on the list.
